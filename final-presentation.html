Slide 5 · Strategy

Lena doesn’t choose a BNPL provider. She takes whichever one the checkout shows her.

Problem hook

She has Klarna and PayPal installed and likes to pay only after she’s decided to keep an item. That is a real need, but today it’s met by the merchant’s checkout, not by anything she owns. Riverty is invisible in the moment of choice (about 6% brand preference), absent in stores, and unreachable for loyal users outside partner merchants.

“Lena wants to decide first and pay second, everywhere she shops. Today that only works where a checkout happens to offer it, and the provider she gets is chosen by the merchant, not by her.”

Value proposition

For Lena: “The Riverty Card puts ‘decide first, pay later’ in your wallet, so you choose when and where to use it, instead of waiting for a checkout to offer it.”

For Riverty: “A direct, everyday relationship with our most loyal users, with interchange on every transaction and interest on revolving balances, without taking volume or margin away from our merchants.”

Data-backed hypothesis

Based on the brief’s observations that Lena uses BNPL on most non-trivial purchases, wants money to leave her account only after she decides to keep an item, takes whichever provider appears at checkout, and cannot use Riverty at non-partner merchants or in stores, we believe that a Riverty card carrying “decide first, pay later” to non-partner shops and physical stores will result in repeated use of the card for purchases she would otherwise make with a debit card, Klarna or PayPal, as measured by at least 40% of activated cardholders making 2 or more off-partner or in-store purchases within 60 days (proposed), and monthly spend per active user trending toward about EUR 333. Guardrails: no decline in partner-merchant checkout volume vs. a matched control group, and arrears and fraud within risk appetite.

Evidence status: the 6% brand preference is a brief data point. Business-case figures (5% conversion, 70% activation, EUR 4,000 to EUR 7,000 spend) are modelling assumptions, not evidence. Thresholds marked “proposed” need Finance and Risk agreement.

Klarna holds its own banking licence and has a head start. Riverty competes on a narrower promise (control over timing for heavy BNPL users), not breadth. In-store rows are interview hypotheses, not findings.

Why Riverty loses her
  • No ownership of the moment of choice. Riverty wins only when a merchant happens to show it.
  • No presence between checkouts. Klarna and PayPal are apps she opens independently.
  • Coverage gap. Outside partner merchants and in stores, Riverty can’t serve her.
  • Heavy users are the most exposed. Each lost purchase hits the best customers hardest.

Evidence: the case brief describes her behaviour. The deck does not quote customer interviews, because none were provided for this case.

Slide 7 · Blueprint

Now, Next, Later: a small test first, scale on evidence

Now · Friends & Family MVP

Virtual card on the Mastercard and Paymentology platform, fixed limit with automatic repayment, visible limit and repayment date, in-app application for existing users, basic servicing, and event tracking.

Features: R2 Virtual card + wallet, R3 What I Owe, R7 Pre-approved invite

Exit gate: activation and repeat use meet agreed thresholds, no decline in partner checkout volume, arrears and fraud within risk appetite, regulatory path confirmed.

Next · Public launch in Germany

Offer to eligible existing Riverty users, physical card if justified, targeted in-checkout and in-app promotion, merchant-facing benefits, and re-test of the 5% conversion assumption.

Features: R4 Fixed limit + auto-repayment, R5 Card controls, R8 Repayment reminders

Exit gate: real conversion and spend support the business case, unit economics positive after credit losses.

Later · Europe & advanced credit

Revolving credit and interest, additional markets (DACH, Benelux, Nordics), and a later review of wallet slots. Each needs its own regulatory approval.

Features: R1 Decide-First Window, R6 Partner discovery

Cut from MVP: R9 Physical card, R10 Rewards / cashback.

Lane placement of R4 depends on Risk and Regulatory approval timing. Effort scores are product judgment, not engineering estimates.

PRD highlights · R3 “What I Owe”

Must have (first sprint)

  • One total amount currently owed on the Riverty card
  • Itemised list of purchases behind the total
  • Next repayment date and amount (depends on R4)
  • Link to the card screen (behind a flag until R2 exists)
  • Home-screen entry point, German copy and EUR formatting
  • Loading, empty and error states, correct and secure data

Won’t have now: keep or return actions, card controls, notifications, partner discovery, other providers’ balances, budgeting, interest and fees, making payments.

Slide 8 · Validation

Experiment plan: does one screen close the information gap?

Hypothesis

Giving cardholders who have made a settled purchase a single “What I Owe” screen will let them state what they owe and when it is due, and feel in control, measured by a +15 percentage point change (30% to 45%, baseline is a placeholder) in the share who correctly state both their amount owed (within EUR 5) and next repayment date at day 14.

Control (A)

Cardholders with at least one settled purchase see a basic list of card transactions only. No single screen showing total owed, the purchases behind it or the next repayment date and amount, and no entry point to one.

Variant (B)

One new screen, “What I Owe”, reachable from the home screen: total owed at the top, itemised purchases (merchant, date, amount), next repayment date and amount. “Owed” means settled purchases only (to be confirmed by Risk and Finance). The card-screen link is not part of this test.

Held constant (isolation check)

App version and release date; card issuing and wallet provisioning (R2); credit limit and repayment mechanics (R4); card controls (R5); partner discovery (R6); invite and onboarding (R7); notifications and reminders (R8); no rewards; Riverty checkout at partners; fraud and risk rules; support routes and scripts; home-screen layout other than the single entry point; the day-14 survey identical in wording and timing in both arms; same population and enrolment period with random assignment. No other feature, copy change or marketing message goes live for either arm.

Primary metric
Share of surveyed cardholders (at least 1 settled purchase) who at day 14 correctly state both amount owed and next repayment date
Baseline
30% (placeholder: replace with the control-arm rate)
MDE
+15 percentage points (30% to 45%)
Sample
163 responders per arm, about 326 enrolled per arm at a 50% response rate. 50/50 split, p < 0.05 two-sided
Secondary
Self-reported control score (4 or 5 out of 5), higher in variant
Guardrail
Monthly spend per active user no more than 10% below control (directional at this size)
Stop rules
Any wrong-balance incident, or 2 or more variant users missing a repayment because of a displayed figure
Ship

Knowledge +15 pts (p < 0.05), control score higher, spend within 10%, no wrong balance, no repayment harm, arms within 50/50 ±5 pts and response rates within 10 pts

Iterate

Knowledge improves but control score or spend is inconclusive

Stop

Any wrong-balance incident, 2 or more repayment-harm cases, or a Risk objection

Inconclusive

Confidence interval spans zero and +15 pts: extend or move to a staged rollout

Read date is fixed at the end of the enrolment and survey window (about 16 weeks at 50 enrolments a week, an assumption to confirm with Analytics). No results are reviewed for success before then. All thresholds are proposals to agree with Finance, Risk and Analytics.

Slide 9 · Launch

GTM: a minimal launch, because the test must stay clean

Goal

Engagement. Cardholders already hold the card, and R3 helps them understand what they owe and when, protecting the churn guardrail and supporting repeat use. R3 doesn’t drive activation by itself, so Conversion would misread it.

Audience

Pilot cardholders with at least one settled purchase. Lena-type users (32, Cologne, salaried, mobile-first, BNPL for non-trivial purchases) are the design target, but pilot membership doesn’t guarantee a match. Only the variant half sees the feature during the test. Employees are analysed separately.

Launch tier: S, minimal

About 1,000 active cardholders reached through Riverty’s own app. Revenue impact is indirect. Marketing during the test would contaminate the A/B experiment. Moving to M requires every criterion in the decision table to hold.

Bad signal to watch for

Any wrong balance: switch the flag off the same day, then Risk review. 2 or more repayment-harm cases: stop and review. Spend more than 10% below control on a 4-week rolling basis: escalate. Survey response below 50% in either arm at week 6: apply the fallback. 3 or more balance-related support contacts from variant cardholders in 4 weeks, or no knowledge difference despite high opens: review copy and layout for the next cycle.

Likely post-launch decision: Iterate. Trigger: the knowledge score rises but spend or the control score is inconclusive. Ship only if every criterion in the decision table holds.

Ownership, budget and timeline

Owners: PM (plan, experiment design, metrics, declaring the gate). Frontend lead (screen, entry point, feature flag, survey prompt). Backend lead (balance endpoint, daily reconciliation, kill switch). QA lead (test plan, release sign-off). Analytics (arm assignment, survey scoring, dashboard, Phase 3 monitoring). UX copy writer (German wording). Legal (copy review). Risk and Compliance (“owed” definition, control-arm approval, stop rules, email consent). R4 product owner (repayment calendar by the gate date). Customer Support (awareness note before the test, FAQ and briefing after). Partner success (merchant note). Budget: no paid media; survey tooling, German copy and legal review, and QA capacity quoted by their owners before Phase 1 ends.

Phase 1, before the gate: R4 repayment calendar delivered, Risk approves the control arm and the “owed” definition, build behind the feature flag with a kill switch and daily balance reconciliation, internal release to the build and QA team only.

Phase 2, weeks 1–16 after the gate: randomise at first settled purchase, day-14 survey in both arms, weekly guardrail review, no other changes to the card journey. Week 4 operational check, week 6 response-rate check, week 8 first descriptive look (safety only). Fallback: if enrolment is below about 200 at week 4 or response is below 50% in either arm at week 6, extend or move to a staged rollout.

Phase 3, weeks 18–21 plus 4 weeks of monitoring: apply the signed decision table. Ship: roll out to all pilot cardholders, send the announcement, brief Support. Iterate: fix and re-run. Stop: switch off the flag. Weeks count from the gate date, when R2, R3 and R4 work together.

Slide 10 · Story

What the work taught me

Friction points
  • Evidence vs. assumption. The business case figures looked like facts until I labelled them as modelling assumptions.
  • Metric that measured a lookup. My first success metric (a knowledge check) could be answered by reading the new screen, so it would win without proving control.
  • Pilot too small. About 1,000 active cardholders can’t power an A/B test on a feature with a modest expected effect.
  • Dependencies. The repayment date needs R4, which sits later in the roadmap, so R3 can’t be tested alone.
Aha moment

Lena isn’t choosing a provider at all. She takes whichever one a merchant’s checkout shows her, so Riverty’s problem is not a better offer but having nothing she owns that carries “decide first, pay later” with her.

It also reframed “What I Owe”: it solves only the Riverty slice of her fragmentation, so it must never promise a full-wallet view.

Key takeaways and what I would do next
  • Write thresholds down before launch, so results can’t be argued afterwards.
  • Pick guardrails that protect the strategy (partner checkout, spend), not just the product.
  • Make the scope smaller to get through approval faster.
  • Interview Lena-type heavy users about in-store behaviour to replace the hypotheses.
  • Get the real baseline, weekly enrolment and survey response rates from Analytics, then resize.
  • Confirm the regulatory path and Risk approval for the control arm. If the pilot is too small, run a staged rollout with the same survey and guardrails.
